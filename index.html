02 / Early BuffettPerspective on scale

Did early Buffett
earn higher returns?

His early partnership record shows what came before Berkshire’s enormous scale.

Buffett’s January 1969 letter reported 31.6% annual compounding for 1957–1968 before the general-partner allocation, and 25.3% for limited partners. Those are 12-year figures, covering a different period and structure from Berkshire.

Read the partnership results, pp. 123–124 ↗

That is the appeal of managing a small sum: a modest opportunity can matter. As capital grows into billions, fewer investments are large enough to move the overall result. Small size can widen the opportunity set; research and judgment still determine what is worth buying.

Buffett on the constraints of scale ↗

In his own words · 2024 annual meeting

“And with a million dollars you could earn 50 percent a year.”

Buffett was describing a hypothetical approach to investing a small sum. This is separate from the reported partnership results above, and is not a return expectation for this fund.

03 / Rule of 72The arithmetic of patience

Ten doubles.
1,024 times.

Double your capital ten times and it becomes 1,024× the starting amount: 210 = 1,024.

At exactly one doubling every three years, that would take 30 years. The Rule of 72 estimates a 24% annual return (72 ÷ 3). Exact annual compounding requires 25.99%; at 24%, each doubling takes about 3.22 years.

What I look for

Opportunities with the potential to at least double, preferably more: multi-baggers, meaning investments that can grow to several times their cost. The aim is substantial upside with limited risk of permanent loss—not an assumption that these investments are low risk.

A mathematical illustration, not a fund target or forecast. Returns vary and can be negative. Fees, taxes, inflation, and cash flows are excluded.

05 / DiversificationLooking at the whole

Different names.
The same bet?

Look at your portfolio. The S&P 500? NVIDIA (NVDA)? AI stocks? “They’re the future.” Who doesn’t already know that?

The investment question is how much of that future is already reflected in the price—and how many of your holdings rely on the same expectations.

I look for undervalued, hated, weird, contrarian, and special-situation ideas: opportunities whose outcomes may depend on something different from the portfolio you already own.

The aim is different return drivers and potentially lower correlation. Being unpopular does not automatically make an investment cheap, safe, or uncorrelated. Those relationships need to be assessed and can change in a sell-off.

A scenario worth considering

The yen carry trade.
What if the tailwind turns?

Borrow yen at low rates, sell yen for dollars, and buy higher-yielding dollar assets: one version of the long-running carry trade. Cheap funding can support demand for those assets.

What if the yen strengthens or funding costs rise? Closing the trade can mean selling assets and buying yen back. That is a scenario to examine, rather than a prediction of an imminent reversal.

BIS research on August 2024 documents how unwinding leveraged currency and equity positions amplified market volatility.
